# Chapter 7: Guinevere and Lancelot

## Scene 1

<!-- paragraph_id: ch7_s1_p1 -->
Lancelot had been gone from court two winters on his quests, and I had almost stopped counting the empty place at the table, when the doors opened one spring noon and he rode into the hall still in his road-dust. He was thinner, and browner, and there was a long new scar across the back of his left hand, but he carried himself as lightly as ever. Knights rose from their benches to clasp his arm, and Arthur laughed aloud and came down from the dais to meet him. I was watching the queen, though I could not have said why, since I had been standing near her, holding a cup for the king. Guinevere had gone quite still. She looked at Lancelot across the whole length of the hall, over all those shoulders and all that noise, the way a person looks at a door she has been told is locked and finds standing open, and for the space of one breath she forgot to hide it. Then Lancelot bowed to her, and she inclined her head, and every face in the hall turned back to the king, and I told myself that I had seen nothing at all.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch7_s1_p2 -->
It was midsummer before I saw them together, and by then I had stopped pretending that I would not look. I had gone out to the walled garden at dusk to be alone with my aching knee, and from the gate I saw the two of them at the far end, walking slowly between the pear trees with a careful yard of air between them. They did not touch. They did not even seem to be speaking, only moving side by side through the long gold light, with the stillness of two people who have already said everything and have nothing left that is safe to say. I could have called out, or coughed, or walked down the path in plain view and ended it, and I knew exactly what a faithful servant would have done. But I loved Arthur, and I loved her, and I loved Lancelot, and no honest course lay open to a man who loved all three. So I stepped back through the gate, closed it without a sound, and went in to supper with my mouth shut, and I have carried that silence ever since.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch7_s1_p3 -->
I came to believe that Arthur knew, though he never said so, and that his not saying so was the heaviest thing he carried. One winter night I could not sleep and went down to the hall for a cup of water, and found one candle burning at the far end and the king sitting alone at the Round Table with his bare hand spread flat upon the wood. He did not look up when I came in. He ran his palm slowly along the grain, as a man might stroke a sleeping dog he is afraid to wake, and his face in the candlelight looked older than his years. I stood in the doorway for a long while, waiting for him to speak, and he never did. At last I went back the way I had come, and I do not think he ever learned that I had been there, or perhaps he did and was grateful I had gone.
