Chapter 1: The Walk to the Lake

I left the hall while the others slept, and the dark outside was colder than any I could remember. My knees ached on the stone steps, and the frost bit through my gloves before I had crossed the yard. Under my arm I carried Excalibur, wrapped in an old grey cloth the way a man might carry a sleeping child. It was lighter than it had ever seemed in battle, and I did not trust that lightness. Somewhere ahead of me lay the lake, and I meant to reach it before I lost my nerve or my breath, whichever failed first.

Halfway down the hill I gave in and sat on a fallen log, setting the bundle across my knees. The wind came through the bare branches in long, low notes, and for a moment it was the old battle horns calling us up the field at dawn. I shut my eyes and saw Arthur as he was when we were both young, his face unlined and a little too serious, his hair stuck flat with sweat after a day of drilling. He had never laughed easily, but when he did it startled everyone, himself most of all. I could hear that laugh now, thin and clear under the wind, and I stayed there until my fingers stopped shaking.

When I stood again I looked down the slope and could not see the water, only a long grey fold of mist where the land ought to end. The lake was farther than I had let myself believe, and my legs, which had carried me through a dozen campaigns, now argued with every step. I was afraid, and I will not pretend otherwise: afraid I would fall on the path and be found by morning with the sword still in my arms, afraid the cold would stop my heart before the work was done. Worse than that, I knew my own weakness. Arthur had asked me twice, and twice I had gone down to the shore and come back with a lie, because the blade was too beautiful to drown. I had pushed it into the reeds and told him I saw nothing but waves. So I made myself a promise, spoken aloud to the empty hillside, that this time I would not hide it among the reeds, however it shone, however my hands begged me to keep it. I would throw it far out, as I was told, and I would not look away until the water closed.

Chapter 2: The Boy in the Courtyard

I first saw him in Sir Ector's courtyard, on a morning when I had ridden in with a message and a sore back. A thin boy of twelve or so was hauling water from the well, two buckets slopping at his sides, while a taller lad leaned on the wall and shouted orders at him. The taller one was Kay, Sir Ector's son, proud of his new spurs, and the boy answered him as a little brother answers, with a grumble and a grin. That was Arthur, though nobody in that yard knew it, least of all Arthur, who believed himself Ector's second son and Kay's to boss about. He saw my horse and forgot his buckets at once, running his hand along its neck and asking its name before he asked mine. I remember thinking he looked at everything as if it might be worth keeping, and that I had never seen a servant's chore done with such gravity.

Before I left that day he caught my sleeve and asked, in a rush, whether I would show him my sword. I should have refused, since it was not my place to arm another man's household, but the pleading in his face was hard to resist. I drew the blade, a plain soldier's sword with a nicked edge, and laid it flat across his two palms. His arms sagged under the weight, and he went red with effort, so I stood behind him and closed his fingers around the grip, moving his wrist until the point rose and held steady. "Thumb along the back," I told him, "and keep your elbow in." Kay, watching from the wall, burst out laughing and called him a scullion playing at knighthood, but Arthur did not so much as glance his way. He kept his eyes on the steel, and when I took it back he looked at his empty hands as if something had been lifted out of them.

At dusk Sir Ector came out onto the steps with a lantern and called the boys in to supper, and I turned to fetch my horse. The lantern light fell across Arthur as he ran past, still holding his hands a little apart, as though the sword's weight lingered in them. Ector did not move or speak, but I saw his face change. It was not anger and not pride; it was an old, patient sorrow, the look of a man watching a loan he has grown fond of and knows he must one day give back. Kay brushed past him and got a pat on the shoulder, while Arthur got only a long, quiet stare. When Ector noticed that I had seen it, he lifted the lantern higher, wished me a safe road, and went inside without another word. I rode out thinking that he knew something about that boy which neither of them had been told.

Chapter 3: Merlin Looks Twice

Merlin came to Ector's house a few weeks later, on a wet afternoon, and I happened to be in the yard again with another letter to deliver. He rode a lean grey mule and wore a grey hood pulled low, so that at first I took him for a travelling friar. Ector met him at the gate with a stiffness I had not seen in him before, and the two men spoke in low voices while the rain ran off their shoulders. The boys had come out to see the stranger, Kay swaggering in front and Arthur half a step behind with a wet rag in his hand. Merlin glanced at Kay and passed over him as one passes a post. Then his eyes met Arthur's, and moved on, and I thought that was the end of it. But he looked back, slowly, the way a man rereads a line of writing he cannot quite believe, and this time he did not look away until Arthur shifted his feet and lowered his eyes.

That evening I stayed for stew, and from my stool at the end of the hall I watched Merlin and Ector bend their heads together by the fire. The logs cracked and spat, and I caught only scraps, but one of them I have never forgotten. Ector said, low and hoarse, that the boy had come to his door as a baby, in the dead of night. Merlin said nothing at all. He only looked into the flames, then across the hall to where Arthur lay asleep on the rushes, and nodded slowly, as if a long account had finally been settled.

At first light I went out to saddle my horse, and found Merlin already waiting by the gate with his mule, as though he had not slept at all. Mist lay in the yard, and somewhere behind the stables a cock was trying out its voice. He laid a thin, cold hand on my arm and drew me a few steps away from the house. "Keep your eyes on that boy," he said quietly. "You will want to remember this house." I asked him what he meant, and he only smiled, a short dry smile with no explanation in it, then climbed onto the mule and rode out into the mist. I stood with the reins in my hand and looked back at the dark windows, and I did what he asked, for the rest of my life.

I had gone a quarter mile down the lane before something made me turn in the saddle and look back. The mist had begun to lift, and there was Merlin, who I thought had left long before me, sitting motionless on his mule at the end of the lane, a grey shape against the pale gate. He was not watching me. His hood was turned toward the yard, where the small figure of Arthur had come out with the buckets and was making for the well, quite unaware of anyone. Merlin stayed like that for as long as I could see him, as still as a heron at the edge of water. A cold, odd feeling settled in my chest, the kind a man gets before weather turns, and I knew, without a single reason I could have given to anyone, that something very large was coming toward that house and that boy.

Chapter 4: The Stone

On New Year's morning the whole country seemed to have crowded into London for the tournament, and before the lists were open every knight in the city had drifted to the churchyard, drawn by a rumour no one could trace. There, in the trampled frost between the graves, stood a great square stone, and fixed upon it an iron anvil, and driven through the anvil to the hilt, a bare sword. Around the hilt ran letters in gold, which a priest had read aloud so many times that he had gone hoarse: whoever draws this sword from the stone and anvil is the rightwise king born of all England. I remember the breath of two hundred men hanging white in the air, and how oddly quiet they were, as men are quiet at a graveside. Some laughed too loudly, some crossed themselves, and some walked round the stone twice with their hands behind their backs, as if the sword might be a trick that would be easier to see from the other side. Nobody had yet dared to lay a hand on it, and I felt, standing in that hush, a small, foolish wish that someone would.

It was then that Kay, who had come up to the churchyard wall to jostle for a better view, clapped a hand to his hip and found it empty. His sword was still lying on the bed at the inn, forgotten in the rush to get to the lists. He swore under his breath, glanced about, and, seeing no one better, shoved Arthur in the back and told him to run and fetch it, and quickly, because the jousting was about to begin. Arthur, now a tall youth of sixteen or so, and no longer carrying buckets but a squire's bundle of Kay's gear, went at once. I watched him thread through the crowd and out of the gate, his breath streaming behind him. Ten minutes later he came back at a trot, red in the face, and told Kay that the door was bolted and the shutters closed; the landlord and his household had all gone off to see the tournament, and no amount of hammering would bring anyone to open it.

Kay cursed him for a fool and turned back to the lists, and Arthur stood a moment with his empty hands hanging, thinking. Then he looked across the street at the churchyard, which had emptied while the knights flocked to the jousting, and I saw the idea reach his face. I had stayed by the gate, half from curiosity, half from a fear I could not name, and so I was the only soul who saw him walk between the graves with that bundle of Kay's gear on his shoulder. He did not hesitate or look around; he set the bundle down, took the hilt in his right hand as I had once shown him, thumb along the back, and drew. The sword slid out of the anvil as if the iron were butter, with a faint, clean sound like a breath, and he stood there holding it in the white morning light, looking more surprised at how easily it came than at anything else. I did not shout or move, for my throat had closed, and standing there by the gate I understood that I had been watching a king being born and that no one else in all England knew.

Arthur brought the sword to Kay, wrapped in his cloak like any ordinary errand, and Kay's face changed as he unwrapped it. He had stood too near the stone that morning to mistake that hilt, and he turned the blade over in his hands, looking from it to the empty anvil and then to Arthur. For once his tongue failed him. He went straight to Sir Ector, who was drying his horse's neck at the edge of the lists, and in a voice I hardly knew he said, "Father, that boy has drawn it. He is the king." Ector looked once at the sword, then at Arthur, and then, to my astonishment, he sank down on both knees in the churchyard mud, and Kay went down beside him. Arthur stood between them, white as the frost, his hands open and shaking. "Please stop," he said. "Get up, both of you. I am only Arthur. I am only your Arthur. Please."

Chapter 5: The Round Table

They crowned him at Easter, and the years after that went by faster than any I had known, in a blur of roads, sieges and quarrels settled at last by treaty. Arthur grew into the crown slowly, as a man grows into a borrowed coat, until one day I looked up and found that the boy from the courtyard had a king's stillness. When he married Guinevere, her father sent a wedding gift that took forty men to carry into the hall: a vast round table, wide enough for a hundred and fifty knights, made of oak so old it had gone nearly black. Arthur walked around it twice with his hand trailing over the wood. "Look at it," he said to me. "It has no head. Nobody can sit above anybody else." I had spent my whole life at long tables where rank was measured by distance from the fire, and I understood at once what he meant, and what it would cost him to make the others understand it too.

The first knights took their places on the feast of Pentecost, and I stood at the door counting them in. Lancelot came first, tall and quiet, with a stranger's reserve, and chose a seat without looking at the others. Gawain followed, red-bearded and loud, clapping men on the shoulder as he went, and then young Percival, who hesitated so long over where to sit that Gawain finally pushed him into a chair and laughed. I had assumed there would be no place for me among such famous names, and I hung back by the wall until Arthur beckoned me over with two fingers. Between a pillar and the great hearth was a chair like all the others, except that along its back, cut cleanly into the black oak, were the letters of my own name. I put my hand on them for a long time, and I do not remember what I said, only that Arthur looked away politely while I said it.

That evening the cooks carried in the first feast, and as the dishes went round I noticed what the rest of us were pretending not to see. On Arthur's right hand stood one chair that no one had taken, and it was no different from the rest except for a pall of silence about it, as if the air around it had gone thicker. Merlin had called it the Siege Perilous, and had said, with the satisfied look of a man who enjoys other people's alarm, that any man who sat there unfit would be destroyed on the spot. Gawain stared at it, then at his cup. Lancelot kept his eyes on the table, and even Kay, who never in his life let a silence stand, found nothing to say. I felt the whole hall lean toward that empty seat, wondering whose name the oak was waiting for, and I looked at Arthur, who was calmly cutting his meat, and understood that he was as curious as any of us and had decided not to show it.

The trouble came as the wine went round a second time. Gawain, flushed and cheerful, said that a king's own nephew ought to sit nearest his uncle, and Kay answered that a king's own foster brother had a better claim than any nephew, and in a breath the two of them were on their feet with their chairs scraping back. Other knights joined in, voices rose, and a cup went over and rolled across the oak, spilling red along the grain.

Then Arthur stood, not hurriedly, and laid his open palm flat on the wood in the middle of the quarrel, and the noise sank as if he had pressed it down. "There is no head here," he said, quite gently. "Look around the table, all of you, and find me one. If you can, I will sit beneath it." Gawain opened his mouth and shut it again, and Kay turned his face aside, red to the ears. The knights looked from one end of that great ring to the other and saw only each other's faces looking back, equal in the torchlight, with nothing above and nothing below. One by one they righted their chairs and sat, and the spilt wine soaked slowly into the oak. In the silence that followed I heard the fire settle in the hearth, and I thought that I had just seen the first law of the realm made without a single word of it being written down.

Chapter 6: The Lady of the Lake

The sword from the stone broke in Arthur's hand during his fight with King Pellinore, snapping a hand's breadth above the hilt, and I will not forget how his face looked as he stood holding half a blade and waiting for the next blow. Pellinore, who had by then forgotten his own quarrel, helped Merlin carry him off the field. Three days later Merlin led us out of the camp, Arthur still pale and quiet, and I followed because I had been told to and had no intention of letting the king ride away from me. We went through dripping woods until the trees opened onto a lake so still that the sky seemed to have been laid flat on the earth. No wind moved on it and no bird skimmed it. Arthur reined in his horse and said, almost to himself, that he had never seen water that was so entirely listening.

Then the middle of the lake began to stir, without a ripple spreading, as if the water were parting before something it knew. A white arm rose out of it, clothed to the fingertips in white silk that did not drip, and in the hand it held a sword upright by the scabbard, the long blade catching a light I could not trace to any sun. I forgot to breathe. Arthur's horse shifted under him and he laid a hand on its neck without looking down, his eyes fixed on that slow, patient arm. "Look well," said Merlin beside him, and his dry voice had gone soft. "That is Excalibur." The arm held still, neither offering nor withdrawing, as if it had all the time in the world and was only waiting to be noticed.

Beside the arm the water rose again, and a woman stepped up out of it as if the lake were a staircase, dressed in white, her dark hair loose and perfectly dry. She did not look at Merlin or at me, only at Arthur, and when she spoke her voice carried across the water as clearly as if she stood at his stirrup. "Take the boat by the alder," she said, "and row yourself out to me. The sword is yours to take, if you will take it as it is given." Close to the bank, half hidden in the branches, lay a small flat boat with a single pair of oars that I would have sworn had not been there a moment before. Arthur dismounted and handed me his reins without a word, and Merlin laid a hand on my shoulder when I made to follow. So I stood on the shore with the horses and watched my king push off alone, rowing with long, careful strokes toward the woman and the white arm, his wake closing smoothly behind him.

When he came back the lake had closed behind him as if nothing had ever stood on it, and the Lady had gone with the white arm. Arthur stepped out of the boat with the sword at his hip and a plain sheath of embroidered leather in his other hand, and in the light on his face I saw, for the first time, the look of a man who has been given more than he asked for. Merlin took the sheath, turned it over, and gave it back. "Guard this better than the blade," he said. "A man who wears it cannot bleed to death, however deep the wound. It is worth ten of the sword." Arthur ran his thumb along the stitching and was quiet a while, then drew Excalibur a hand's breadth, watched the light run down it, and slid it home. "It was lent to me," he said. "I know that much. And one day, when I have done with it, I will bring it back to her."

Chapter 7: Guinevere and Lancelot

Lancelot had been gone from court two winters on his quests, and I had almost stopped counting the empty place at the table, when the doors opened one spring noon and he rode into the hall still in his road-dust. He was thinner, and browner, and there was a long new scar across the back of his left hand, but he carried himself as lightly as ever. Knights rose from their benches to clasp his arm, and Arthur laughed aloud and came down from the dais to meet him. I was watching the queen, though I could not have said why, since I had been standing near her, holding a cup for the king. Guinevere had gone quite still. She looked at Lancelot across the whole length of the hall, over all those shoulders and all that noise, the way a person looks at a door she has been told is locked and finds standing open, and for the space of one breath she forgot to hide it. Then Lancelot bowed to her, and she inclined her head, and every face in the hall turned back to the king, and I told myself that I had seen nothing at all.

It was midsummer before I saw them together, and by then I had stopped pretending that I would not look. I had gone out to the walled garden at dusk to be alone with my aching knee, and from the gate I saw the two of them at the far end, walking slowly between the pear trees with a careful yard of air between them. They did not touch. They did not even seem to be speaking, only moving side by side through the long gold light, with the stillness of two people who have already said everything and have nothing left that is safe to say. I could have called out, or coughed, or walked down the path in plain view and ended it, and I knew exactly what a faithful servant would have done. But I loved Arthur, and I loved her, and I loved Lancelot, and no honest course lay open to a man who loved all three. So I stepped back through the gate, closed it without a sound, and went in to supper with my mouth shut, and I have carried that silence ever since.

I came to believe that Arthur knew, though he never said so, and that his not saying so was the heaviest thing he carried. One winter night I could not sleep and went down to the hall for a cup of water, and found one candle burning at the far end and the king sitting alone at the Round Table with his bare hand spread flat upon the wood. He did not look up when I came in. He ran his palm slowly along the grain, as a man might stroke a sleeping dog he is afraid to wake, and his face in the candlelight looked older than his years. I stood in the doorway for a long while, waiting for him to speak, and he never did. At last I went back the way I had come, and I do not think he ever learned that I had been there, or perhaps he did and was grateful I had gone.

Chapter 8: Morgan and Mordred

Morgan le Fay came to court in the autumn, Arthur's half-sister, smiling as if she had come only for the pleasure of seeing her brother's house. She kissed Arthur on both cheeks, praised the Round Table, and complimented the queen on her gown, and everybody in the hall seemed warmed by her except me. I have spent my life among liars and I know the look of one who enjoys it, and there was too much patience in her smile, and a quickness in her dark eyes as they travelled around the room, counting and pricing what they saw. She stayed three weeks, and in all that time I never once saw her ask a question that was not an easy one. On the morning she left, Arthur went to take up his scabbard from its place beside his bed and found only the hook. The plain embroidered sheath that could not let him bleed was gone, and with it, I have come to think, the last of the easy years.

Mordred came to the Round Table the following spring, a young knight with an open face and an easy laugh, and within a month half the court was in love with him. He was Arthur's son, got on Morgause in a year the king preferred not to discuss, and he wore that fact lightly, as a man wears a handsome cloak he has never been taught to be ashamed of. He remembered every servant's name, carried Gawain's shield without being asked, and listened to old knights tell the same stories as though each were new. I sat across from him at supper many nights and never caught him in a fault. What I did notice, because I had learned to notice such things, was where his eyes went when he thought no one was watching. They went to the high seat at the head of the hall, the one place no one had ever been allowed to sit, and they rested there a little longer, and a little more fondly, than any loyal man's should.

Morgan came again at midwinter, supposedly to pray at the chapel, and on her second night I found out what she had really come for. I was crossing the cloister after the late bell when I heard low voices on the other side of a pillar, and I stopped in the shadow of the arch before I had decided whether to. It was Morgan, wrapped in a hooded cloak, and with her, bareheaded and attentive, the young knight everyone loved. "Tell it at the Easter feast," she said, "when the hall is full and the wine has been round. A thing like that is only dangerous when it is said before witnesses." Mordred asked, in his pleasant, reasonable voice, whether anyone would believe him, and she laughed softly and said that Lancelot's own scar would be proof enough, and the queen's face would do the rest. They spoke of Guinevere and of Lancelot as one speaks of a lock and a key, and when they parted I stayed against the cold stone long after their footsteps had faded, my heart going like a fist on a door.

In the end it was Agravain who sprang the trap, a week before Easter, and he did it with the grim pleasure of a man who has waited years to be proved right. He sent word that the king was away hunting, and Lancelot, who should have known better, went to the queen's chamber, and Agravain was waiting in the passage with a dozen armed knights and Mordred a step behind him, smiling as if it grieved him. They beat on the door and called Lancelot a traitor by name. He came out through them with no armour and a borrowed sword, and I heard afterward how many he had put down on the stairs before he reached the courtyard, and how he vaulted onto a horse that was not his and rode into the dark without once looking back. The queen was found where she had been left, white and upright, her hands folded in her lap. When Arthur returned that night he was told everything in the hall before he had taken off his gloves, and I watched him take it, one word at a time, and understood that tomorrow he would have to judge the woman he loved, by the very law he had made.

Chapter 9: Camlann

The war with Lancelot ended the way such wars end, with nobody having won it. Arthur had carried it overseas, grimly and without joy, and had hardly settled his army before a messenger rode in with salt on his boots and the news that Mordred, left to guard the kingdom, had called himself king and taken the queen's hall as his own. Arthur read the letter twice, folded it, and gave the order to march, and I have never heard so many men go silent at once. We crossed in the grey of a late spring and marched home through a country that was already choosing sides, and by the time we saw Mordred's banners drawn up on the plain at Camlann I think every man in our host knew there would be no one left to bury the dead. Arthur rode along our line that evening on his old horse, with Excalibur at his hip and no scabbard beside it, looking at each face as though he meant to remember it.

At dawn a truce was agreed, and the two armies stood back a long bowshot from each other with the bare plain between them, so quiet that I could hear a lark somewhere above the mist. Arthur had ridden out to meet Mordred in the middle, and he had commanded every man on both sides that if a single sword were drawn, the trumpets should sound and the day should be lost to everyone. I stood at his stirrup with my hand on my reins and my eyes on Mordred's far line, and for an hour I began to hope. Then, a little to my left, a young knight cried out and looked down, and I saw a thin brown adder slide away through the grass from his boot. He did what any frightened man does. He drew his sword to kill it, and the blade flashed once in the grey light, and across the plain a hundred men saw it. For a moment nothing moved. Then the trumpets began to blow, first on one side and then the other, and all that hope went out of me like water from a cracked jar.

There is no way to tell the noise of a battle to anyone who has not stood in it. It rose from the whole plain at once, iron on iron and iron on wood, and under it the long wet sound of horses screaming, and the dust went up in a yellow cloud so thick that I fought half the morning with a sleeve over my mouth. I lost sight of Arthur twice and found him again each time by the glint of Excalibur, rising and falling like a lamp carried through a burning house. Close beside me fought Iolo Pryce, a quiet Welsh knight who had shared my watch more nights than I could count and who had never once complained of the cold. I saw the spear take him under the arm. He looked at me with mild surprise, said something in his own language that I did not catch, and slid from his saddle into the trampled grass, and I could not reach him, for the press carried me on. By the time the light began to fail, the plain was no longer a field but a floor of the dead, men and horses together, and the survivors stood among them as though they had walked into a house in which they no longer knew anyone.

At nightfall Arthur and I walked out among the dead, because he would not rest until he had seen who was left. A thin moon had come up, and by its light the plain looked like a field after floodwater, strewn with shapes that might have been men or fallen banners. We stepped carefully. Now and then a hand moved or a voice asked for water, and Arthur knelt each time, spoke quietly, and rose again with that closed look he wore when he could do nothing. He had gone perhaps a hundred paces beyond the last of our fires when he stopped so suddenly that I nearly walked into him. A little ahead of us, upright among the bodies, someone was leaning on a broken spear with both hands, the way an old man leans on a staff at the end of a long road. The helmet was gone, the fine hair was matted dark with blood, and still there was no mistaking the easy shoulders, or the face, which turned slowly toward us and tried to smile. It was Mordred, and he was alive.

Arthur said his name once, very quietly, and Mordred answered by letting go of the spear and drawing the sword at his belt, and then they were on each other before I could move. I have gone over those few heartbeats a thousand times and have never been able to slow them down. I saw Arthur take the broken shaft from the ground, saw him step inside the other's guard and drive it home, and saw Mordred, spiked through and still upright, force himself along the wood until he stood face to face with his father. Then his sword came down on the side of Arthur's head with the last strength in his body, and he fell back on the grass and did not stir again. Arthur stood a moment, swaying, and then his knees gave way, and I caught him before he struck the ground. The blow had cut through the helmet and deep into the skull, and the blood poured over my hands warm and fast, as it would not have done if a plain embroidered sheath had still been at his side. I laid him down against my knees under that cold moon, and he looked up at me and tried to say something, and the plain around us was so quiet that I could hear the blood falling on the grass.

Chapter 10: The Lake

Arthur spoke at last when I had lifted him as far as the edge of the trees, where there was a little shelter from the wind. His voice was so thin that I had to bend my head to his mouth to hear it. He told me that his time was done, that there was nothing left in the world that I could do for him but one thing, and that I must do it now, before the dawn. I was to take Excalibur, and go down to the water, and throw it in as far as my arm would send it, and then come back and tell him what I saw. I said I would not leave him lying on the open ground, and he almost smiled, and reminded me, as a king reminds a servant, that he had made a promise to someone beside a lake a very long time ago. So I took him up in my arms, light as a man can be who has given everything away, and carried him a little way toward the shore, until a small chapel by a ruined hermitage gave me somewhere to lay him down. Then I drew the sword from his belt and went out into the cold.

The shore was no more than a quarter hour away, a shelf of grey stones and reeds with the water lying black and flat beyond them, and I walked down to it with the sword held out before me. At the water's edge I stopped, for the dawn was coming up behind the far hills, and the first pale light ran along the blade from hilt to point. I had carried that sword through half my life and never once looked at it as I looked at it then. It was clean as a mirror, in spite of everything it had done that day, and the stones in the hilt burned like coals in a hearth that nobody had told to go out. I thought of it lying at the bottom of the lake in the cold and the dark, and my arm would not lift. Twice I raised it, and twice it fell, until at last I knelt and pushed Excalibur deep into the reeds where the mud would close over it. Then I climbed back up the path with the water running out of my boots, and when Arthur asked me what I had seen, I looked him in the face and told him I had seen nothing but waves.

Arthur looked at me for a long moment and said, quite gently, that I had not told him the truth, for waves would have made a sound when a sword like that went in. He sent me back, and I went down again with my teeth set, and stood at the edge a second time, and a second time I could not do it; I hid the blade under the bank and returned with another tale of wind and water. This time he did not whisper. He raised himself on one elbow with the old force in his face and called me a faithless man, who for the sake of a bright thing would let his king lie dying with his last command undone. I have been shamed by many people in my life and by many battles, but never so deeply as by that. I ran down to the shore for the third time, dragged Excalibur out of the mud and the reeds, and before I could argue myself out of it again, I swung it round my head and flung it as far out over the lake as I could.

I watched the sword turn over twice in the air, flashing, and I had time to think that it would be quick, and clean, and that nobody would ever know what I had nearly done. It never reached the water. Out of the dark middle of the lake a white arm rose, in the same white silk I had seen on a morning forty years before, and a hand opened at the end of it and closed around the hilt as easily as a woman takes a cup from a table. It brandished the blade three times against the paling sky, slowly, with a sound like a long breath being let go, and every stone on the hilt flared and went out. Then the arm drew back beneath the surface, taking Excalibur with it, and the water closed over the place with no ring and no bubble, and where the sword had been there was nothing at all. I stood on the shore with my empty hands still raised, and the lake lay as flat as a sheet of dark glass, and the whole world seemed to wait with me for something that would not come again.

When I came back up the path to the chapel, a barge was moving across the lake out of the mist, low and black, with no oars that I could see and no sail, and in it sat three queens in dark hoods, their faces turned toward the shore. They did not speak to me. Two of them rose and lifted Arthur between them as if he weighed nothing, and one of them, whose face I knew from long ago, laid his head in her lap and bent over him until her hair hid his eyes. He turned his head once and looked past her at me, and I think he said something, but the wind took it. Then the barge slid out into the light, smaller and smaller, until the mist closed behind it and the water kept it. I stood there until the sun cleared the hills, and then I turned and began the long walk back up the frosted hill, alone, to the place where I had started, with the cold in my knees and nothing in my arms at all.
