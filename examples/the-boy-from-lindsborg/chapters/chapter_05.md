# Chapter 5: The Argonne

## Scene 1

<!-- paragraph_id: ch5_s1_p1 -->
On the night of 25 September 1918 the men waited in the dark near Charpentry. No one was allowed a fire, and few of them spoke above a whisper. Everett sat with his back against a bank of wet earth and his rifle across his knees, and listened to the far-off thud of the guns rolling over the hills. Somewhere ahead of him lay the ground they would cross in the morning, and he could not see any of it. He thought of the wheat at home, standing still under the same sky, and he held that thought as steadily as he could until the order to move came down the line.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch5_s1_p2 -->
At dawn on the 26th the barrage opened, and the ground itself seemed to shake. The sound was so great that Everett felt it in his chest before he understood it with his ears. A thick fog lay over the fields, and when the order came the men climbed out and went forward into it, each man keeping the shape ahead of him in sight. Shells burst somewhere in the whiteness, and voices called out and were lost. Everett kept walking, one boot after the other, with the pressed flower in his Bible riding against his chest and no way of knowing what waited in the fog.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch5_s1_p5 -->
Near Exermont the fighting grew fierce, and the noise was beyond anything Everett had imagined. Guns thundered from every side, bullets cracked overhead, and the air was full of smoke and flying dirt. He could not hear the orders shouted a few feet away, so he did what he had learned to do and kept close to the man beside him. They went forward together in short rushes, dropped flat, got up, and went on, each of them watching the other. Neither said much, but every time Everett looked over, the man was still there, and that was what got him through the morning.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch5_s1_p3 -->
By the end of that first day Everett was sitting in a shell hole with his knees drawn up, too tired to feel much of anything. The guns had slackened, and in the gray evening the men around him sat or lay where they had stopped, speaking little. He took out his Bible and held it in both hands, not opening it at first, only feeling the worn cover under his thumbs. Then he turned to the place where the pressed prairie flower lay, looked at it a moment, and closed the book again. He was alive, and it would have to be enough for the night.
