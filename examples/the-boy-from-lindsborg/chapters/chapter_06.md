# Chapter 6: The Pressed Flower

## Scene 1

<!-- paragraph_id: ch6_s1_p1 -->
The fighting stopped at eleven o'clock on the morning of 11 November 1918, and for a while Everett hardly knew what to do with the quiet. Men stood about in the open and looked at one another, and some laughed and some said nothing at all. But the war's end did not send them home. There were long winter weeks of waiting in cold billets, with drills and inspections and endless rumors about ships. Everett wrote to Karin and Anders and to Linnea, and he counted the days by the mail that came. When at last his turn arrived it was already spring, and the long road back to Kansas began.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch6_s1_p2 -->
In the spring of 1919 the train brought Everett back into Lindsborg, and the first thing he saw through the window was the green of new wheat in the fields. Karin, Anders and Linnea were at the station, standing close together. Karin reached him first and held on to his coat without a word, and Anders took his bag from him as if nothing could have been more natural. Linnea waited her turn, and when it came she only said his name. Everett found that he had nothing ready to say to any of them, so he stood still and let them look at him, a year older and back where he had begun.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch6_s1_p3 -->
Everett went back to the same land, and he farmed it for the rest of his life, until 1971. He plowed in the fall, sowed the wheat, watched the sky through the dry weeks and cut the grain when it turned gold, as his father had taught him. He did not talk about the war, not to his family and not to the neighbors, and no one in the house thought to ask twice. His Bible stayed on a shelf with the pressed prairie flower inside it and the postcard from Charpentry, and what he had seen in France went quietly into the soil of the farm. The seasons turned over the fields year after year, and he was there for every one of them.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch6_s1_p4 -->
Now the Bible lies open on the great-niece's table, with the pressed prairie flower still tucked between its pages and the postcard from Charpentry beside it. She turns the card over in her fingers and thinks of the child in the rain, and of the boy who put it inside his coat. Then she takes up the old photograph once more, the young soldier in his uniform and hat, stiff in the studio and trying so hard not to smile. She smiles for him instead. Everett never told anyone his story, so she has set it down herself, for the family that came after him and for the one who will read it next.
