# Chapter 6: The Pressed Flower

## Scene 1

<!-- paragraph_id: ch6_s1_p1 -->
On the eleventh of November, 1918, Everett was not wounded, only worn out, so tired that his bones felt hollow. He sat against a bank of cold earth with his rifle across his knees, waiting, and at eleven o'clock the guns stopped. The silence came all at once, so large and strange that for a moment he thought he had gone deaf. Then he heard a bird, one small bird somewhere in the ruined trees, and beside him a man began to laugh, and then to weep, and Everett sat very still and let the quiet wash over him.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch6_s1_p2 -->
In the spring of 1919 Everett came home by train, watching the Kansas country slide past the window, flat and green and wide open under a huge sky. At the station in Lindsborg his father Olof was waiting on the platform in his Sunday coat, hat in his hands. Neither of them hurried. When Everett stepped down, Olof looked at him for a long moment, then gripped his shoulder hard and said, in Swedish, only that it was good to see him, and the two of them walked out to the wagon together without another word.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch6_s1_p3 -->
He farmed that same land for the next fifty-odd years, until 1971. Each autumn he drilled the wheat, each June he watched it turn, and each summer he brought it in, season after season, in good years and lean ones, while the neighbours' sons grew old beside him. He did not talk about the war. He kept the Bible on the shelf in the front room, and when anyone asked about France he looked out at the weather and spoke instead about rain.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch6_s1_p4 -->
Years later, his great-niece opened his old Bible and found, pressed between its pages, a faded prairie flower, pale as paper, and a small postcard from a French village called Charpentry. She held them in her palm and sat quite still. The silence he had kept all his life was not coldness, she saw now. It was where he had put everything he could not say: the wheat he missed, the quiet he had come home to, the village he had walked through in the mud. She looked once more at the boy in the photograph, trying so hard not to smile, and understood him at last.
