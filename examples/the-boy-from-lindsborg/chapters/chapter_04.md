# Chapter 4: Crossing

## Scene 1

<!-- paragraph_id: ch4_s1_p1 -->
In the spring of 1918 the division was ordered out of Camp Doniphan, and Everett packed the same bag he had carried in. The men filled the train cars with kit and songs and loud talk, and the prairie slid past the windows for days until it gave way to cities and then to the gray edge of the sea. In the harbour a great ship waited, dark and heavy against the water, with lines of soldiers climbing its gangways like ants. Everett found his place in the line, shifted his bag to his other shoulder, and walked up into the hold. Behind him lay Kansas and Oklahoma and everything he knew, and ahead of him lay a country he had only seen on a map.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch4_s1_p2 -->
Out on the Atlantic the ship pitched and rolled, and half the men aboard were sick. Everett, who had never seen more water than a farm pond, spent two gray days hanging over the rail with his stomach turned inside out. Below deck the air was thick and sour, and the hammocks swayed together in the dark. But by the third night he could stand again, and in the evenings the men gathered wherever there was room and sang, the old hymns and the army songs and the songs they all knew from home. Everett did not sing much, but he listened, and the voices carried over the black water long after the lamps were put out.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch4_s1_p3 -->
They landed in France under a low, wet sky and marched inland with their packs on their backs. The road was churned to mud, and the column slopped through a village where the stone houses stood close together and children watched from doorways. Everett felt strange to be walking in a country where every sign was in a language he did not know, even the Swedish he knew did not help him here. At the edge of the village he saw his first French farm, a small stone house with a barn and a few fields hemmed in by hedges. It was nothing like the wide open wheat of Lindsborg, but a woman was feeding chickens in the yard, and for a moment he felt almost at home.

<!-- paragraph_id: ch4_s1_p4 -->
Later the division moved up toward the front, and it rained for most of the march. The men walked with their collars turned up and their boots full of water, and the guns grew louder with every mile. At a halt beside the road a small French child came out of a doorway and pressed a postcard into Everett's hand, then ran back inside before he could say a word. The card was a little damp at the corners, and it showed a village street with a church and a row of low houses. On the back was a single name in faded ink, Charpentry, a place Everett had never heard of. He tucked it inside his coat to keep it dry, and the column moved on into the rain.
