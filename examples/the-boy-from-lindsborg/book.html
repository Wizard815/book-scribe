Chapter 1: The Photograph

His great-niece found the photograph in a shoebox at the back of an old drawer, under a rubber-banded bundle of receipts and a card from some forgotten Christmas. It was a studio portrait, brown at the edges, and in it a young man stood very straight in his uniform and hat, his hands at his sides, his chin lifted as if someone had told him to hold still and he had taken the order seriously. He was trying not to smile. She could see the effort in the corners of his mouth, a small struggle the camera had caught and kept for more than a hundred years, and she turned it over and read the name on the back: Everett Lund.

She knew him only from the family's talk. Old Everett had farmed wheat outside Lindsborg all his life, they said, a quiet man who kept his opinions to himself. Nobody remembered him ever speaking of the war.

She sat with the photograph a long while under the lamp, wondering what he had been thinking that day. A farm boy from Kansas, in a borrowed-looking stillness, in a studio far from any wheat field, about to be sent across an ocean he had probably never seen. Was he proud, or afraid, or only anxious to keep his face still until the photographer said he could move?

Chapter 2: Wheat and Draft Cards

The Lund farm lay a few miles outside Lindsborg, where the wheat ran flat and gold to the edge of the sky. Olof and Freja had come from Sweden to work that land, and they worked it from first light to last. Their son Everett was up before dawn every morning, pulling on his boots in the dark kitchen, then crossing the cold yard to feed the animals and carry water while the wheat stood pale and silent all around him and the east slowly turned grey.

On the fifth of June, 1917, Everett drove into Lindsborg with the other young men of the county to register for the draft. They stood in a long, shuffling line outside the hall, hats in their hands, joking too loudly and then falling quiet as the line moved forward. Everett said little. He was thinking of the wheat, ripening now toward harvest, and of his father Olof out in the fields with only the hired hands and a bad hip, and he wondered who would bring the crop in if the army came for him.

That summer he made up his mind before anyone could make it for him, and joined the Kansas National Guard. He told his father at the kitchen table after supper, and Olof listened without a word, turning his coffee cup slowly on its saucer. Freja stood at the stove with her back to them. When Olof finally spoke it was only to say, in Swedish, that the wheat would wait, and Everett understood that this was as close as his father could come to saying he was proud.

The night before he left, Freja packed his canvas bag herself, folding each shirt twice and tucking wool socks into the corners, and would not let him help. When there was no room left for anything else she went to the shelf, took down the family Bible, and pressed it into his hands. Everett began to say it was too heavy to carry, but she closed his fingers around it and held them there, and whatever she meant to tell him stayed in her own language, unspoken.

Chapter 3: Camp Doniphan

Camp Doniphan was dust. It rose from the parade ground under a thousand marching boots and settled in the mess lines, in the blankets, in the coffee. Everett stepped down from the train with his canvas bag and his mother's Bible and found rows upon rows of canvas tents stretching away across the flat Oklahoma ground, and hundreds of boys from Kansas and Missouri milling between them, farm boys and clerks and store boys, all of them shouting to each other in the same flat plains voices he had grown up with.

Winter came to the camp early, and the drilling went on through it. Everett marched in the dark before breakfast with his breath smoking and his fingers too numb to feel the rifle, and learned to load it, aim it and fire it at paper targets on the far hillside, one careful round at a time. A sergeant with a voice like a barn door followed the line shouting at every mistake, and most of all at Everett, whose boots would not keep step. "Lund!" he bellowed. "You march like a man behind a plough!" Everett said nothing, and lifted his boots higher.

On Sunday, when the drilling stopped, Everett sat on his bunk with a borrowed pencil and wrote a letter to his mother. He told her the food was plentiful and the boys were decent, and he did not tell her about his cold hands or the sergeant. What he could not put into words was how he missed the quiet of the farm: the hush of the yard before sunrise, the wind moving through the wheat, the whole wide stillness that he had never known to be a thing until the camp's noise took it away. He wrote "Your son, Everett," and sat a while looking at the line.

The night before the train east, the barracks was loud with boys packing and joking, and Everett slipped outside to sit alone on an upturned crate. He opened the Bible on his knee and found the pressed prairie flower lying between the pages, flat and brittle, its colour faded to the pale shade of old paper. He held it by its stem, hardly breathing, as though it might crumble at a word. It still smelled faintly of the pasture at home, and for a moment, under the cold Oklahoma stars, he was back there.

Chapter 4: Crossing

In the spring of 1918 the 35th Division sailed for France, and Everett went with it, packed into the hold of a crowded troopship. Soldiers filled every deck, shoulder to shoulder at the rails, sleeping in rows between the hatches, and the air smelled of wet wool, tobacco and engine oil. Everett, who had never in his life been farther from land than a Kansas stock pond, was seasick before the coast was out of sight. He gripped the rail, green and miserable, while the grey water heaved beneath him and a thousand men around him tried not to look at one another.

Near the coast of Europe the ship began to zigzag, swinging first one way and then the other, and word went round the decks that they had entered the submarine danger zone. Everett wedged himself against a bulkhead, held his mother's Bible against his chest, and prayed without making a sound. A boy from Missouri crouched beside him, pale and freckled, and after a while he dug an apple from his coat, bit it in half with a crunch, and handed the larger piece over. Neither of them said a word, and the apple tasted like somewhere else entirely.

They landed in a grey harbour under a low grey sky, and the French people were waving. Women in shawls, old men and small children lined the quay and called out to the soldiers, holding up bread and flowers, and Everett, stiff-legged from the voyage, waved back without knowing what to say. Then came the long train, slow and rattling, carrying them east through unfamiliar fields and villages toward the training area in the Vosges, where the hills rose dark with pine and the war, for the first time, began to feel near.

Chapter 5: The Argonne

On the night of the twenty-fifth of September, 1918, the division moved up in the dark. There was no moon to speak of, and no talking, only the shuffle of boots on a churned road and the clink of tin cups against canteens. Far ahead the guns were already at work, and the whole horizon flickered orange and white like a summer storm that never broke. Everett walked with his rifle on his shoulder and his Bible buttoned inside his coat, feeling each distant thud in the soles of his feet, and marched on toward the Meuse-Argonne with the men of the 35th.

At dawn on the twenty-sixth the whistles blew and the line went forward into smoke. The ground was torn and sodden, and mud sucked at every boot, so that each step took a deliberate effort, and shells burst ahead and behind in gouts of black earth. Everett kept his head low and his eyes on the back of the man in front, one stubborn pace after another, toward the village of Charpentry, which lay somewhere beyond the haze. He could hear nothing but the roar of the guns, and he thought of nothing at all except the next step.

By midmorning they had tumbled into a muddy trench, and Everett found himself pressed against the wall beside the boy from Missouri, who had shared his apple on the ship. They crouched there with water around their ankles while the shells went over. Everett dug into his pocket and found the last heel of his bread, grey and damp, and broke it in two. The boy took his half with a nod, and the two of them chewed in silence, listening to the war move on above their heads.

When the line moved again, they came into Charpentry, or what was left of it. Roofs had fallen in, walls stood open to the sky, and the street was a litter of broken beams and glass. Among the rubble of a ruined house Everett saw a small rectangle of card, face up, and bent to pick it up. It was a postcard, a picture of the village as it had once been, whole and quiet in the sun. He wiped the mud from it with his sleeve, and slipped it carefully between the pages of his Bible.

Chapter 6: The Pressed Flower

On the eleventh of November, 1918, Everett was not wounded, only worn out, so tired that his bones felt hollow. He sat against a bank of cold earth with his rifle across his knees, waiting, and at eleven o'clock the guns stopped. The silence came all at once, so large and strange that for a moment he thought he had gone deaf. Then he heard a bird, one small bird somewhere in the ruined trees, and beside him a man began to laugh, and then to weep, and Everett sat very still and let the quiet wash over him.

In the spring of 1919 Everett came home by train, watching the Kansas country slide past the window, flat and green and wide open under a huge sky. At the station in Lindsborg his father Olof was waiting on the platform in his Sunday coat, hat in his hands. Neither of them hurried. When Everett stepped down, Olof looked at him for a long moment, then gripped his shoulder hard and said, in Swedish, only that it was good to see him, and the two of them walked out to the wagon together without another word.

He farmed that same land for the next fifty-odd years, until 1971. Each autumn he drilled the wheat, each June he watched it turn, and each summer he brought it in, season after season, in good years and lean ones, while the neighbours' sons grew old beside him. He did not talk about the war. He kept the Bible on the shelf in the front room, and when anyone asked about France he looked out at the weather and spoke instead about rain.

Years later, his great-niece opened his old Bible and found, pressed between its pages, a faded prairie flower, pale as paper, and a small postcard from a French village called Charpentry. She held them in her palm and sat quite still. The silence he had kept all his life was not coldness, she saw now. It was where he had put everything he could not say: the wheat he missed, the quiet he had come home to, the village he had walked through in the mud. She looked once more at the boy in the photograph, trying so hard not to smile, and understood him at last.
