Chapter 1: The Photograph

It began with a photograph, an old one, soft at the edges and gone the color of weak tea. His great-niece turned it over in her hands and found a young man looking back, her great-uncle Everett Lund, long before he was anyone's great-uncle at all. She had known the name all her life, the way a family knows the names of people who were there before it. Now she held his face, and she wanted to know the boy behind it. He came from a wheat farm near Lindsborg, Kansas, the son of Swedish parents, and somewhere between that flat country and the photograph was a story nobody had yet written down. She meant to write it.

In the picture Everett stands in a photographer's studio, in his uniform and his hat, held as stiff as a fence post. He was having it taken before he sailed, and he plainly knew that it mattered. His mouth is pressed flat, and you can see him working at it, trying with all his might not to smile. Whatever he was feeling, the boy from the wheat farm kept it behind his face and let the camera have only the uniform.

Ruth wanted very much to learn who the boy in the photograph had been. The family had kept his name, but the person behind it had slipped away, and she was determined to bring him back. Sitting with the picture in her lap, she tried to imagine the town he came from, and she pictured the church bells of Lindsborg ringing across the wheat on a Sunday morning. She felt sure he had heard them as a child, standing in the yard in his good clothes, long before any uniform. It was a small thing to imagine, but it was where she chose to begin.

Chapter 2: Wheat and Draft Cards

Everett grew up on wheat. The farm outside Lindsborg lay open to the sky on every side, and as a small boy he learned the year by the color of the field: green and short in spring, then rippling and pale, then gold and ready for the harvest. His father, Anders, who had crossed the ocean from Sweden with little more than his hands, taught him to walk a row without trampling the stalks and to rub a head of wheat between his palms and judge it by the taste of the grain. His mother, Karin, kept the kitchen and the household going, and there were chores for a boy before school and again after it, so that Everett's earliest memories were of work shared with the two of them. Nobody on that farm talked much about feelings, but a boy could read his father's pride in a nod over a straight furrow, and his mother's in the extra helping set in front of him at supper.

At home the family spoke Swedish. Anders said grace in it before supper, and Karin answered him, and Everett learned the words for bread and rain and bedtime in Swedish first. On Sundays they drove into Lindsborg for church, where the hymns were sung in the old language. At school on Monday it was English, and Everett moved between the two without thinking about it.

In June 1917 the men of the county went to register for the draft, and Everett was one of them. He came home with the card in his pocket and the knowledge that his number might be called in the weeks ahead. He did not want to sit on the farm and wait to be sent for, so he decided to join the Kansas National Guard instead. He told his parents at supper. Anders listened and said little, but Karin put down her fork and asked him whether he was sure, and she asked it twice. Everett said he was, and she nodded, and went on watching him across the table long after the plates were cleared.

On the day Everett left for Camp Doniphan, Linnea Sjöberg was waiting on the platform. She stood a little apart from the other families, with her hands folded in front of her, and she did not wave until he had found a seat by the window. He could not hear what she said through the glass, so he lifted his hand and she lifted hers. The train jerked, then rolled, and the station and the town of Lindsborg slid backward. Everett kept his eyes on her until the shape of her was gone, and then he sat very straight and looked at the flat country passing, which was the only home he had ever known.

Chapter 3: Camp Doniphan

Camp Doniphan rose out of the Oklahoma prairie like a town that had been put up overnight. When Everett stepped down from the train he walked into a haze of dust, kicked up by boots and wagon wheels and the wind that never seemed to rest. Row after row of tents ran off across the flat ground, their canvas gone the color of the earth, with the first rough wooden barracks still being hammered together among them. Men called to one another in a dozen accents, and Everett heard Missouri voices and Kansas ones, and none of them spoke Swedish. He shouldered his bag, tasted grit on his tongue, and understood that this was where he would become a soldier.

The drilling began at once. Everett and the other new men marched up and down the dusty parade ground with wooden rifles on their shoulders, since the real ones had not yet come, and a sergeant barked at them until their steps fell together. In the weeks that followed they dug trenches in the hard ground, practiced with the bayonet and learned how the guns and the infantry were meant to work together. Everett was a farm boy and used to long days, so the work did not frighten him, though his hands blistered in new places. At night he lay on his cot in the tent and listened to the wind pulling at the canvas.

One evening Everett borrowed a pencil and wrote home on a sheet of paper balanced on his knee. "Dear Mother and Father," he wrote. "I am well and the food is plenty. It is hot here and the dust gets in everything, but I am getting used to it. The men are good fellows, Kansas and Missouri both. We drill all day and I sleep like a stone at night. Please do not worry, Mother. Tell Linnea I am thinking of her. Your son, Everett." He read it over twice, saw that he had left out almost everything, and decided that was how his parents would want it.

When winter came to Oklahoma it came hard, with a cold wind off the plains that found every crack in the barracks walls. The men slept in their coats and socks, and the stove at the end of the room never kept up with the cold. On those nights Everett sometimes took out his Bible and opened it carefully to the place where the pressed prairie flower lay. It had gone flat and papery and pale, but it still held its shape, and it still smelled faintly of Kansas. He did not read much, only looked at it for a while in the dim light, then closed the book and tucked it back under his blanket.

Chapter 4: Crossing

In the spring of 1918 the division was ordered out of Camp Doniphan, and Everett packed the same bag he had carried in. The men filled the train cars with kit and songs and loud talk, and the prairie slid past the windows for days until it gave way to cities and then to the gray edge of the sea. In the harbour a great ship waited, dark and heavy against the water, with lines of soldiers climbing its gangways like ants. Everett found his place in the line, shifted his bag to his other shoulder, and walked up into the hold. Behind him lay Kansas and Oklahoma and everything he knew, and ahead of him lay a country he had only seen on a map.

Out on the Atlantic the ship pitched and rolled, and half the men aboard were sick. Everett, who had never seen more water than a farm pond, spent two gray days hanging over the rail with his stomach turned inside out. Below deck the air was thick and sour, and the hammocks swayed together in the dark. But by the third night he could stand again, and in the evenings the men gathered wherever there was room and sang, the old hymns and the army songs and the songs they all knew from home. Everett did not sing much, but he listened, and the voices carried over the black water long after the lamps were put out.

They landed in France under a low, wet sky and marched inland with their packs on their backs. The road was churned to mud, and the column slopped through a village where the stone houses stood close together and children watched from doorways. Everett felt strange to be walking in a country where every sign was in a language he did not know, even the Swedish he knew did not help him here. At the edge of the village he saw his first French farm, a small stone house with a barn and a few fields hemmed in by hedges. It was nothing like the wide open wheat of Lindsborg, but a woman was feeding chickens in the yard, and for a moment he felt almost at home.

Later the division moved up toward the front, and it rained for most of the march. The men walked with their collars turned up and their boots full of water, and the guns grew louder with every mile. At a halt beside the road a small French child came out of a doorway and pressed a postcard into Everett's hand, then ran back inside before he could say a word. The card was a little damp at the corners, and it showed a village street with a church and a row of low houses. On the back was a single name in faded ink, Charpentry, a place Everett had never heard of. He tucked it inside his coat to keep it dry, and the column moved on into the rain.

Chapter 5: The Argonne

On the night of 25 September 1918 the men waited in the dark near Charpentry. No one was allowed a fire, and few of them spoke above a whisper. Everett sat with his back against a bank of wet earth and his rifle across his knees, and listened to the far-off thud of the guns rolling over the hills. Somewhere ahead of him lay the ground they would cross in the morning, and he could not see any of it. He thought of the wheat at home, standing still under the same sky, and he held that thought as steadily as he could until the order to move came down the line.

At dawn on the 26th the barrage opened, and the ground itself seemed to shake. The sound was so great that Everett felt it in his chest before he understood it with his ears. A thick fog lay over the fields, and when the order came the men climbed out and went forward into it, each man keeping the shape ahead of him in sight. Shells burst somewhere in the whiteness, and voices called out and were lost. Everett kept walking, one boot after the other, with the pressed flower in his Bible riding against his chest and no way of knowing what waited in the fog.

Near Exermont the fighting grew fierce, and the noise was beyond anything Everett had imagined. Guns thundered from every side, bullets cracked overhead, and the air was full of smoke and flying dirt. He could not hear the orders shouted a few feet away, so he did what he had learned to do and kept close to the man beside him. They went forward together in short rushes, dropped flat, got up, and went on, each of them watching the other. Neither said much, but every time Everett looked over, the man was still there, and that was what got him through the morning.

By the end of that first day Everett was sitting in a shell hole with his knees drawn up, too tired to feel much of anything. The guns had slackened, and in the gray evening the men around him sat or lay where they had stopped, speaking little. He took out his Bible and held it in both hands, not opening it at first, only feeling the worn cover under his thumbs. Then he turned to the place where the pressed prairie flower lay, looked at it a moment, and closed the book again. He was alive, and it would have to be enough for the night.

Chapter 6: The Pressed Flower

The fighting stopped at eleven o'clock on the morning of 11 November 1918, and for a while Everett hardly knew what to do with the quiet. Men stood about in the open and looked at one another, and some laughed and some said nothing at all. But the war's end did not send them home. There were long winter weeks of waiting in cold billets, with drills and inspections and endless rumors about ships. Everett wrote to Karin and Anders and to Linnea, and he counted the days by the mail that came. When at last his turn arrived it was already spring, and the long road back to Kansas began.

In the spring of 1919 the train brought Everett back into Lindsborg, and the first thing he saw through the window was the green of new wheat in the fields. Karin, Anders and Linnea were at the station, standing close together. Karin reached him first and held on to his coat without a word, and Anders took his bag from him as if nothing could have been more natural. Linnea waited her turn, and when it came she only said his name. Everett found that he had nothing ready to say to any of them, so he stood still and let them look at him, a year older and back where he had begun.

Everett went back to the same land, and he farmed it for the rest of his life, until 1971. He plowed in the fall, sowed the wheat, watched the sky through the dry weeks and cut the grain when it turned gold, as his father had taught him. He did not talk about the war, not to his family and not to the neighbors, and no one in the house thought to ask twice. His Bible stayed on a shelf with the pressed prairie flower inside it and the postcard from Charpentry, and what he had seen in France went quietly into the soil of the farm. The seasons turned over the fields year after year, and he was there for every one of them.

Now the Bible lies open on the great-niece's table, with the pressed prairie flower still tucked between its pages and the postcard from Charpentry beside it. She turns the card over in her fingers and thinks of the child in the rain, and of the boy who put it inside his coat. Then she takes up the old photograph once more, the young soldier in his uniform and hat, stiff in the studio and trying so hard not to smile. She smiles for him instead. Everett never told anyone his story, so she has set it down herself, for the family that came after him and for the one who will read it next.
