Chapter 1: The Photograph

The photograph of Everett is old now. The paper has gone soft at the corners and the picture has faded to the brown of weak tea, but he is still in it, waiting to be looked at. Everett Lund of Lindsborg, Kansas, born in the spring of 1896, was a young man when it was taken, and this is where his story begins.

Everett's parents were Swedes. Nils Lund was born in 1860 in Småland, and Sigrid Nilsson two years after him, and the two of them crossed an ocean with their first son, Olof, to make a life on the Kansas prairie. They settled near Lindsborg, where other Swedish families had already put down roots, and Nils took up the farming of wheat and corn. Everett was born there in 1896, a boy of the new country, and grew up between his parents' old language and the wide flat land they had chosen.

Chapter 2: Wheat and Draft Cards

By the summer of 1917 Everett was twenty years old and worked his father's land, the wheat and the corn, on the rural route outside Lindsborg. He was single, and no wife or child depended on him. On the fifth of June, the day the whole country was told to register for the draft, he put his name to a Selective Service card, and under his trade he wrote the plain word farmer. It was a small piece of paper, and it was the first one that asked him to belong to something larger than the farm.

After the registering came the waiting. Everett's card carried an order number of its own, 1388, and in the weeks that followed he could do nothing with it but go on working. The local board would decide who could be spared, and farm work was a common reason to be passed over, though many farm sons were called all the same. So he waited, and did the work of the season, not knowing which kind of farm son he would turn out to be.

Chapter 3: Camp Doniphan

In the end the call came, and Everett left the farm for Camp Doniphan, on the edge of Fort Sill in Oklahoma. The 35th Division was being put together there out of National Guard units from Missouri and Kansas, and a Kansas farm boy of Swedish parents found himself among thousands of strangers who spoke the same flat American as his neighbours and little like his mother's kitchen. Olof stayed behind with the wheat, and Lindsborg was a long way north. Everett was a soldier now, and the army was about to teach him what that meant.

The camp was a new city of tents and drill grounds, built in a hurry for an army that did not yet exist, and Everett spent the months there learning to be one man among many. The division sailed for France in the spring of 1918, and by autumn a different enemy was moving through the camps on both sides of the ocean. The influenza that came in September, carried along by troop movements and crowded ships, struck young men hardest. Everett was one of those young men. Some of the boys around him went to the hospital, and he did not.

Chapter 4: Over There

In the spring of 1918 the division crossed the Atlantic. For a boy who had grown up on land so flat that the horizon was the only fence, the ocean was a new kind of emptiness, and the troopship carried more men than he had ever seen in one place. His parents had made this crossing in the other direction once, toward Kansas, and now their son was going back across the same water, a soldier in an American uniform bound for France. When the ship at last made land, the division went on to train in the Vosges, still some way behind the guns.

On the fourteenth of October, somewhere in France, Everett wrote to his mother. He told her that he was well and hoped she and his father were the same, and that he had not had a dry sock since the end of September. He could not say where the division was, but he sent her a postcard from the village of Charpentry, which he said had been a pretty place once. He told her not to worry about the wheat, because Olof would manage it. The flu was going through the camps, he wrote, and some of the boys had gone to the hospital. He kept the flower she had pressed in his Bible, and every night he read the psalm she had marked. Then he asked her to tell his father that he had not forgotten what he said at the station, and he signed himself her loving son.

Chapter 5: The Return

When the war was over, Everett came home to Lindsborg and went back to the farm. The land was the same land, the wheat and the corn that Olof had kept going in his absence, and he took up the work as if he had only stepped away from it for a season. He did not leave it again.

In 1919 Everett married Elsa Berglund, a Lindsborg girl born in the last autumn of the old century, and together they raised two children on the farm, Karl, born in 1921, and Margit, born in 1924. He died at home on the second of March, 1971, at the age of seventy-four, and the services were held at Messiah Lutheran Church in Lindsborg. The newspaper called him a farmer and a veteran. Those who knew him remembered the collection of pressed flowers he kept, and they remembered that he never talked about the war.
