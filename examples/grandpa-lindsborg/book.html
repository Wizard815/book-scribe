Chapter 1: The Photograph

The old photograph is of Everett Nils Lund, who was born on 9 March 1896 in Lindsborg, Kansas. His father was Nils Lund and his mother was Sigrid Nilsson, and he had one brother, Olof.

His parents, Nils Lund and Sigrid Nilsson, came from Småland in Sweden. Nils was a farmer of twenty-four and Sigrid was twenty-two when they arrived in New York on 21 May 1884, aboard the SS Wieland, with their son Olof, who was one year old, and a destination of Kansas. They settled near Lindsborg, where Everett was born twelve years later.

Chapter 2: Wheat and Draft Cards

On 5 June 1917, the day men across the country registered at their local draft boards, Everett filled in his registration card. He gave his name as Everett Nils Lund, his age as twenty, his birth date as 9 March 1896, and his home as Rural Free Delivery Route 2, Lindsborg, McPherson County, Kansas. He wrote that he was a farmer of wheat and corn, employed by his father, Nils Lund, and that he was single, with no one solely dependent on him for support. A lottery drawn in Washington on 20 July would decide the order in which men were called, and although farm work was a common reason for deferment, many farm sons were called anyway.

The card records that he already served in the Kansas National Guard, where he had enlisted in 1917, and that he did not claim exemption from the draft. The McPherson County Board gave it the serial number 412 and the order number 1388.

Chapter 3: Camp Doniphan

The 35th Division was formed in 1917 from National Guard units of Missouri and Kansas, and Everett, who had enlisted in the Kansas National Guard that year, served in it. The division trained at Camp Doniphan, at Fort Sill in Oklahoma, before it sailed for France in the spring of 1918.

When the division left Camp Doniphan, it went overseas, and in the spring of 1918 Everett's unit sailed for France. There the division trained in the Vosges, and by the end of September it would be in the Meuse-Argonne Offensive.

Chapter 4: Over There

On 14 October 1918 Everett wrote to his mother from somewhere in France. He told her he was well and hoped she and his father were the same, that he had been a long time in the mud and rain, and that he had not had a dry sock since the end of September. He could not say where they were, but he named the village on the postcard he sent with the letter, Charpentry, which he said had been a pretty place once. He told her not to worry about the wheat, because Olof would manage it, and he mentioned that the flu was going through the camps and that some of the boys had gone to the hospital. He had kept the flower she pressed in his Bible, he wrote, and read the psalm she had marked every night, and he asked her to tell his father that he had not forgotten what he said at the station.

For the 35th Division the offensive went badly from the start. It entered the fight on 26 September 1918, the first day, and attacked toward Exermont and Charpentry, fighting near Vauquois and Exermont, and it suffered heavy losses. The offensive went on until 11 November, and the village of Charpentry, which Everett named on his postcard, lay in the path of that first attack.

Chapter 5: The Return

Elsa Berglund was born in Lindsborg, Kansas, on 21 September 1899, and she died in Lindsborg on 8 December 1984, at the age of eighty-five, thirteen years after Everett.

Everett died at home on 2 March 1971, at the age of seventy-four. The Lindsborg News-Record called him a farmer and veteran, and wrote that after the war he returned to the farm and worked the same land for more than fifty years. He was survived by his wife, Elsa Berglund, and by their children, Karl and Margit, and by four grandchildren, and services were held at Messiah Lutheran Church in Lindsborg.

In 1919 Everett married Elsa Berglund, and their son Karl was born in 1921 and their daughter Margit in 1924. The obituary says he was known for his pressed-flower collection, and the family record notes that he kept a pressed prairie flower in his Bible.
