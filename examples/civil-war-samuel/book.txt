Samuel's War

Author’s note

This book tells the story of my great-grandfather, Samuel Thomas Whitfield, a soldier from Licking County, Ohio, who fought in the Civil War. It is built upon the foundation of family documents and historical records, including the archives of the 76th Ohio Infantry Regiment, papers from the Whitfield Family, Samuel Whitfield’s 1911 obituary, his letter from Ringgold in 1863, his 1890 pension application, and his 1862 Muster In Card. While these documents provide the framework of Samuel’s life and service, the scenes, conversations, and inner thoughts presented here are imagined, an attempt to bring to life the experiences of a man who, like so many others, lived through an extraordinary time.

Chapter 1: The Farm on the Licking

The late winter wind bit at Samuel’s ears as he and his father, John, mended a stretch of fence. The ground was still too hard to turn, but the days lengthened, and the sap would run soon. “Pa,” Samuel said, his breath pluming in the cold air. “I’m going to Newark. To enlist.” John straightened up, his movements stiff with age and labor. He didn’t look at Samuel, but at the barren fields stretching toward the Licking River. “The farm needs you, son,” he said, his voice flat as the winter sky.

Samuel Whitfield Tintype 1862

Shep, a shaggy farm dog, nudged Samuel’s hand. John finally turned, his eyes holding a glint of steel. “Your brother Daniel is still young. Your mother worries enough. This war isn’t a summer picnic. Men don’t come back the same.” Samuel kicked at a frozen clod of earth. “It’s my duty, Pa. Everyone’s going. It’s not right to stay here.”

The morning Samuel left, the air still held the bite of winter. Hannah, his mother, met him at the farm gate, her hands clasped. “Mind yourself, Samuel,” she said, her voice a whisper against the cold. “And remember us.” He nodded, his throat tight, then turned to the road. The walk to Newark was long, the fields stark under a pale sky. At Camp Sherman, the air buzzed with men and the smell of damp earth and woodsmoke. He stood in line, a farmer no more, and wrote his name. Five feet, nine inches tall, twenty years old, a new soldier.

Chapter 2: Ringgold Gap

Charge at Ringgold Gap, 1863 (an illustration)

The roar of cannon fire was a physical blow, shaking the ground beneath Samuel’s feet as the 76th Ohio surged across the open field. “Close up, Whitfield!” a sergeant bellowed, his voice raw. “Keep those colors up!” But even as he spoke, the flagbearer staggered, the regimental banner dipping, then falling to the frozen earth. Samuel lunged forward, his gaze fixed on the smoking ridge ahead. A searing blow to his left arm, just above the elbow, spun him around. He stumbled, clutching the wound, the world blurring as he fell.

He landed hard, the shock running through him even as the pain in his arm flared. Dust and grit filled his mouth. Above him, the battle raged, a terrifying symphony of shouts, musket fire, and the screams of men. He tried to push himself up, but his left arm hung useless, his fingers numb. The sergeant’s face, grim and determined, appeared through the haze. “Get back, soldier! Get back!”

Days later, the camp near Ringgold was a muddy, somber place. Samuel, his arm bandaged and slung, sat by a smoldering fire, a pen clutched awkwardly in his right hand. “I take my pen to let you know I am alive and mending,” he wrote, the words a thin comfort against the memory of the gap. He thought of Daniel, sick with fever in Chattanooga, and the walk along the canal by the mill. “Wait for me if you can,” he finished, his own hope a quiet prayer in the cold December air.

Chapter 3: Daniel

The air in the Chattanooga hospital ward in March of 1864 was thick with the smell of sickness and old bandages. Samuel sat beside Daniel’s cot, his brother’s face pale and slick with sweat. Daniel’s breath was a shallow rasp, and his eyes, when they fluttered open, seemed to look through Samuel, back to a place far away. “The Licking,” Daniel whispered, his voice faint. “Remember… the fishing there? Old Shep, chasing the ducks.” A weak smile touched his lips. “Pa always said we’d have that back forty cleared by spring.”

Daniel’s hand, thin and hot, found Samuel’s. “Look after Ma, Sam,” he murmured, his grip surprisingly strong. “Tell little Ruth… tell her about me. What it was like.” His eyes closed, and for a long moment, only the ragged sound of his breathing filled the silence. Then, with a sigh that seemed to empty him, it stopped. The date was March 9, 1864.

That November, the Georgia pines blurred past, a monotonous green as Samuel marched with Sherman’s army toward the sea. The rhythmic crunch of boots on the road was a constant thrum, broken only by the occasional shout or the distant crack of a foraging party’s rifle. Beside him, Joseph Hanna, another man from Company B, chewed on a piece of hardtack. “Remember Daniel?” Joseph said, his voice low. “Always talking about that back forty. Said he’d clear it by spring.” Samuel nodded, the memory of his brother’s last words a fresh ache. The scent of woodsmoke and pine needles filled the cool autumn air, a stark contrast to the hospital ward.

Chapter 4: Coming Home

The summer air in July 1865 hung warm and heavy, thick with the scent of honeysuckle and damp earth. Samuel, finally home, walked with Martha along the canal, the familiar path by the old mill now lush with green. The water, a placid ribbon, reflected the clear blue sky, broken only by the occasional ripple of a jumping fish. “I thought of this walk often,” Samuel said, his voice a little rough, the words feeling strange after so long. Martha squeezed his arm, her smile soft. “I always hoped you would come back to it, Sam.”

On a crisp October day in 1866, Samuel and Martha were married at the Second Presbyterian Church in Newark. Martha, radiant in her best blue dress, met Samuel at the altar. Afterwards, as the small gathering mingled outside, Hannah embraced Martha. “He would have liked this,” Samuel’s mother whispered, her eyes, still prone to tears, glancing toward the distant horizon. “Daniel, I mean. He always did love a good wedding.” Martha squeezed Hannah’s hand, a silent promise passing between them as the autumn wind rustled through the tall oak trees.

Years later, on the farm near Newark, Thomas, a sturdy boy, helped Samuel with the heavier chores, while Clara Belle, a bright-eyed girl, assisted Martha. Little Joseph, barely five, chased chickens in the yard. Samuel, his left arm still stiff from the old wound, struggled with the pump at the well. Martha came up beside him, her hand gently guiding his over the cold iron. “Here, let me,” she said, her voice soft. “It catches sometimes, doesn’t it?” Samuel nodded, a faint ache in his shoulder. “Just a reminder,” he said, a wry smile touching his lips. “Of Ringgold Gap.”

Chapter 5: The Old Soldier

The August air in 1890 was thick with the hum of cicadas and the scent of Martha’s baking. Samuel sat at the kitchen table, his left arm resting on the worn wood, a sheaf of papers before him. Joseph Hanna, his old Company B comrade, leaned over the table, a pen in his hand. “This new act,” Joseph said, tapping the document, “says you need ninety days service, honorable discharge, and can’t do manual labor.” Samuel nodded, flexing his stiff arm. “Manual labor is about all I’ve known, Joseph. But this arm…” He trailed off, the memory of Ringgold Gap a phantom ache. “Remember the mud that winter, after the Gap? Seems like a lifetime ago.”

Years later, the Grand Army of the Republic post in Newark was a familiar comfort. Samuel, his hair now thin and white, sat among his fellow veterans, the air thick with pipe smoke and old stories. Younger men, sons and grandsons of those who had fought, would sometimes drift into the meetings, their eyes wide with curiosity. “Tell us about the war, Mr. Whitfield,” a young man once asked, his voice earnest. Samuel would clear his throat, his gaze drifting to the faded flag in the corner. “It was a long time ago,” he’d say, “and it wasn’t much like the stories in the papers.”

On January 3, 1911, the winter light was weak in the house on Granville Street. Samuel lay in his bed, his breath shallow, Martha’s hand in his. His eyes, cloudy with age, found hers. “Remember that walk, by the canal?” he whispered, his voice thin. “After I came home.” Martha nodded, tears welling. He closed his eyes, a faint smile on his lips, the memory a private warmth against the cold January air.
